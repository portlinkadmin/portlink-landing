The problem

This is what coordination looks like today.

A single port call involves 17+ stakeholders, 3 time zones, and an average of 283 emails over 6 weeks.

Critical updates get buried in reply-all chains. Version control means "Final_v3_REVISED_USE_THIS.xlsx". Handoffs between shifts rely on sticky notes and institutional memory.

When something goes wrong — a delayed berthing, a last-minute excursion change, a customs hold — there's no single place to see what happened, who was notified, or what the current status is.

The industry knows this is broken. But the cost of change has always been higher than the cost of chaos. Until now.

Process

How it works

Four steps. One system. Complete coordination.

Workflow

Capture
Assign
Approve
Audit

Step 1

Capture every requirement

Port call data flows in from cruise lines, port authorities, and service providers into structured templates. No free-text emails — every piece of information has a field, a type, and a timestamp.

→ Vessel specs, berth preferences, provisioning needs, excursion manifests, customs declarations — all captured once, visible to all authorized parties.

Step 2

Assign owners to every action

Each task in a port call gets an owner, a deadline, and a status. The port agent coordinates, the cruise line approves, and the service provider delivers — all in the same workspace.

→ "Gangway position" → Oslo Port Authority → Due: 48h before arrival → Status: Pending

Step 3

Approve with one click

Changes require explicit approval from the right stakeholder. No more "I assumed that was confirmed." Every approval is logged, timestamped, and attributed.

→ Berth 14-A confirmed by Oslo Port Authority (K. Hansen) — 2026-03-04 09:14 UTC

Step 4

Audit everything, automatically

Every action, change, and decision is recorded in an immutable log. When auditors, insurers, or regulators ask what happened — you have the answer in seconds, not days.

→ Full port call history exportable as JSON, CSV, or PDF. GDPR-compliant retention policies built in.
